Closed-loop inference for robot runtime adaptation

The hands are fine. The decisions are not.

A frozen manipulation policy solves 37% of single-step kitchen tasks. Ask it to chain those same steps toward a goal it has not seen, and it solves 0.7%.

Nothing about its motor skill changed between those two numbers. What changed was the amount of deciding required. So we left the weights alone and rebuilt the loop around them.

The problem, stated once
Given a manipulation policy whose weights are frozen after training, and a deployment stream whose instructions, viewpoints, embodiments and goals drift away from that training distribution, find the inference-time process — the loop that decides what to look at, what to attempt next, which controller should attempt it, and whether it worked — that maximises the probability of satisfying the task predicate within a bounded interaction budget. The optimisation variable is the loop, not the weights.

No gradient is taken anywhere in this project. Adaptation happens once per episode, is written down, and can be undone.

Why the loop is the right variable
Evidence

The skills are already in the weights; the compositions are not. A policy at 37% on atomic tasks and 0.7% on unseen compositions is not short of motor skill.

Economics

Retraining needs new robot data, is global, and cannot be rolled back for one task. A runtime costs no data, is local to an episode, and is reversible.

Diagnosis

Every failure the loop records has a name: wrong object, wrong order, wrong moment to stop. Weight-space failures do not come with names.

Challenge

Four ways a loop around a frozen policy quietly fails.

Each one is cheap to build and expensive to notice.

What happens

Re-detect the scene on every turn and the objects get new names every turn. "The bowl I moved two steps ago" cannot be said, because nothing survives between turns to say it about.

What it costs

An occluded object is not hidden, it is deleted. Progress cannot be tracked symbolically, so it has to be re-derived from pixels inside the planner's context — the slowest, least reliable, most expensive place to put it.

What happens

Show the after-image back to the same model that chose the action and ask whether it worked.

What it costs

Judgement error is correlated with decision error, so the loop cannot bound its own optimism. It stops early, declares success on an unfinished task, and pays a full multimodal call per step for the privilege.

What happens

Findings such as "this policy drops negations" or "this verb almost never succeeds" get measured once and frozen into a prompt as constants.

What it costs

They are true, and they are knowledge about one policy on one benchmark. Swap either and the prompt is confidently wrong. A gain built on them is partly the gain from an expert having already studied the benchmark.

What happens

A hosted frontier model is called once per decision, and a second hosted model is called to locate objects.

What it costs

Money per trial and seconds per step, neither of which a robot control loop has. It also makes the result unreproducible for anyone without those two keys.

Insight

Five moves, each borrowed from a field that solved a version of this already.

Borrowed from

Supervisory control, which splits a slow decision loop from a fast tracking loop and lets each be designed for what it is good at.

Consequence

Weight space is not the only place adaptation can live. The runtime is a second channel: closed-loop, per-episode, reversible, and carrying about ten numbers per situation instead of a billion.

Borrowed from

Multi-target tracking: gate by distance, match by cost, and keep the track when the measurement goes missing.

Consequence

Identity becomes a property of the whole history instead of the current frame. Object #3 is the same physical thing all episode — after it moves, after it is picked up, after it disappears. Only then can anything be checked rather than re-argued.

Borrowed from

Class-agnostic segmentation, where finding an object does not require knowing its category.

Consequence

Every physical thing on the table gets a row whether or not we have a word for it, so a missing row means genuinely not visible rather than merely unnamed. Naming happens downstream, so a wrong guess is revisable and never corrupts the state estimate.

Borrowed from

Runtime assertion checking in safety-critical software, where a sound checker answers "cannot decide" rather than guessing.

Consequence

Three answers, not two: verified, refuted, cannot tell. Hard verdicts wherever geometry decides; only the third answer goes back to the planner. Strictly stronger where it applies, and never weaker where it does not.

Borrowed from

Risk-averse contextual bandits: be pessimistic where evidence is thin, and price the time a slow arm costs you.

Consequence

The loop learns which controller suits which kind of subgoal from outcomes, described only by features that carry across robots — verb class, whether the target is named by a relation, measured object shape, clutter, and how many times we have already tried here. Retrying and escalating fall out of that last feature instead of being written as a rule.

New in how we measure, not only in what we build

Sweep the policy

Prior work varies the planner and holds the policy fixed. We hold the loop fixed and vary the policy. It is the only way to learn whether a gain belongs to the loop or to one checkpoint.

Report Λ, not Δ

Raw point gains are not comparable across benchmarks with different starting points. Λ is the share of the remaining headroom the runtime recovered, so 78% → 92% and 6% → 36% can be read on one scale.

Declare the mode

Learning during evaluation is test-time adaptation. Cold, calibrated and online are run separately and never merged into a zero-shot claim.

Architecture

Four stations and one rule: nobody grades their own work.

Click a station to see what it owns. Then step an episode through all four.

Controller A — analytic

A self-calibrating Cartesian servo: hover, descend just below the object's top surface, close, lift. It measures its own command-to-motion gain during the episode, so the same code drives a different control rate or a different arm without retuning. It never claims a placement worked — that is the checker's job.

Controller B — the frozen policy

The learned policy, run on one short literal subgoal at a time for a bounded number of steps. It never sees the full task. It reports typed outcomes — budget exhausted, no motion, server error — because "ran out of time" and "never moved" mean completely different next moves upstairs.

    What the planner never sees

    No benchmark name, no object list, no claim about what this policy is bad at. The same prompt is valid for every policy we test — that is what makes the policy sweep meaningful.

    Why escalation is not a rule

    How many times we have already tried is part of the situation the scheduler scores. After a refuted attempt the analytic arm's estimate drops and the learned arm wins on its own.

    Why disappearing can be success

    For containment, an object vanishing under a container's rim is evidence for the claim, not against it. The checker handles that case explicitly; a picture-based judge usually calls it a failure.

    How the numbers were taken
    Paired

    Both arms run the same tasks from the same benchmark initial states, so every comparison is paired trial by trial rather than sampled independently.

    Unmodified predicate

    Success is the benchmark's own predicate. The runtime's verdicts are logged for audit but never substituted for it.

    Λ, defined

    Λ = Δ ÷ (100 − baseline): the share of what was still missing that the runtime recovered. It lets a 94% axis and a 0.7% split be compared honestly.

    Cost
    Per trial

    No API spend. The planner is open weights on one local GPU, the policy servers on two more, four simulators on the rest — one allocation, everything inside it. The marginal cost of a trial is the GPU time it occupies.

    Per decision

    The checker replaces one multimodal call per step with a geometric test, and the persistent registry replaces re-describing the scene in tokens. Both were built for reliability; both happen to be what makes an 8B planner viable.
